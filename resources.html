Plain-language guides for a safer household.

Short, practical reading on home cybersecurity and digital privacy — published slowly, and only when genuinely useful. These guides educate; they don't replace a personal assessment of your household.

Practical guides

Start with these.

If a message asks for money, gift cards or a bank change

Most household fraud arrives as a convincing message — a "family member" in trouble, a "bank" warning, a "contractor" with new payment details. The defence is a habit, not software:

  1. Pause. Urgency is the tell. Real organizations and real family members can wait ten minutes.
  2. Contact the person another way. Call a number you already have — never one supplied in the message itself.
  3. Agree on a family rule. For example: no money moves on the strength of a text or email alone. Ever.
  4. If money has gone, act in hours, not days. Call your bank's fraud line first, then report to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre at 1-888-495-8501.

Build a one-page household emergency list

If an account is taken over or a phone is lost, the first hour goes better when nobody has to guess. One printed page, kept somewhere safe:

  • Your bank's fraud line — the number on the back of your card.
  • Email and Apple/Google account recovery pages — where to go to lock things down.
  • Your phone carrier's line — to freeze a lost phone or a hijacked number.
  • The Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre — 1-888-495-8501.
  • A trusted family contact — the person everyone calls when a message seems off.

Helping a parent stay safer online: where to start

Adult children are often the first to notice a parent is exposed. The goal is protection that respects independence:

  1. Start with a conversation, not a takeover. Ask what they've seen — strange calls, confusing messages — and listen first.
  2. Secure the email account first. It's the key that resets everything else. Strong password, multi-factor sign-in, working recovery options.
  3. Agree on one money rule together. "We always phone each other before money moves" protects everyone, not just parents.
  4. Write down the emergency numbers and keep them by the phone — bank, carrier, Anti-Fraud Centre.
  5. Make it ongoing. A short check-in every few months beats one big intervention.

Six phone settings worth checking tonight

  1. Automatic updates on — for the phone itself and your apps.
  2. A real screen lock — six digits or more, not a four-digit birthday.
  3. App permissions — which apps can see your location, microphone and contacts? Remove what isn't needed.
  4. Find-my-device enabled — so a lost phone can be located, locked or erased.
  5. Multi-factor sign-in on your Apple ID or Google account — the account that rules the phone.
  6. Review what backs up where — know whether your photos and messages are backed up, and to which account.
